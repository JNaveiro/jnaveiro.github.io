Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

One of the essential techniques for computing integrals of functions of one variable was changing variables (also known as $u$-substitution). In essence, when computing an integral of the form $\int_a^bf(x)\,dx$, it is sometimes useful to write $x$ as a function of an auxiliary variable $u$, say $x=\varphi(u)$. The change of variables formula then says that \[ \int_{a}^{b}f(\varphi(u))\varphi'(u)\,du = \int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)} f(x)\,dx. \]

There is a two-dimensional version of the change of variables formula for integration, but this formula is beyond the scope of this course. For the purposes of the course, we will content ourselves with discussing a particular change of variables for double integrals: changing to polar coordinates.

Recall that if $(x,y)$ is a point in the plane, then the polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ of $(x,y)$ are related to the (Cartesian/rectangular) coordinates $(x,y)$ by the equations \[ \begin{aligned} r&=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\ x&=r \cos \theta,\\ y&=r \sin \theta. \end{aligned} \] The picture on the right describes the relationship between $(x,y)$ and $(r,\theta)$.

Integration over Polar Rectangles

Suppose that we are given a continuous function $z=f(x,y)$, defined on a polar rectangle of the form \[ R=\big\{(r,\theta)\mid a\leq r\leq b,\,\alpha\leq \theta\leq\beta\big\}. \] We will assume furthermore that $0\leq \beta-\alpha\leq 2\pi$. Our objective is to compute the integral \[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA. \] To do this, we will first partition $R$ into smaller (polar) subrectangles. More specifically, we choose a sequence \[a=r_0 < r_1 < \cdots < r_m= b \] where the distance between each two terms is $\Delta r=(b-a)/m$, together with a sequence \[\alpha=\theta_0<\theta_1<\cdots<\theta_n=\beta\] where the distance between each two terms is $\Delta\theta=(\beta-\alpha)/n$.

We have thus partitioned $R$ into subrectangles of the form \[ R_{ij}=\big\{(r,\theta)\mid r_{i-1}\leq r\leq r_i,\,\theta_{j-1}\leq \theta\leq\theta_j\big\}. \] Observe that the area of $R_{ij}$ is \[ A(R_{ij})=\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\cdot \pi(r_{i}^2-r_{i-1}^2) =\frac{r_{i-1}+r_i}{2}\Delta r\,\Delta\theta. \] Now, for each rectangle $R_{ij}$, choose $(\overline{r_{i}},\overline{\theta_{j}})$ to be the center point of $R_{ij}$, with respect to polar coordinates: \[ \overline{r_{i}}=\frac{r_i+r_{i-1}}{2},\quad \overline{\theta_{j}}=\frac{\theta_j+\theta_{j-1}}{2}. \] Then, an approximation of $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$ is \[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA \approx \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n A(R_{ij})f(\overline{r_i}\cos\overline{\theta_j},\overline{r_i}\sin\overline{\theta_j}) = \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n \overline{r_i}f(\overline{r_i}\cos\overline{\theta_j},\overline{r_i}\sin\overline{\theta_j})\Delta r\, \Delta \theta. \] As we make $n,\,m\to\infty$, these approximations get closer to the value of our integral: \[ \lim_{m \to \infty\\ n\to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n \overline{r_i}f(\overline{r_i}\cos\overline{\theta_j},\overline{r_i}\sin\overline{\theta_j})\Delta r\, \Delta \theta = \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA. \] However, from the definition of double integral, we see that the right hand side is also equal to \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{m \to \infty\\ n\to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n \overline{r_i}f(\overline{r_i}\cos\overline{\theta_j},\overline{r_i}\sin\overline{\theta_j})\Delta r\, \Delta \theta =& \int_\alpha^\beta\int_a^b rf(r \cos \theta,r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta \\=& \int_a^b\int_\alpha^\beta rf(r \cos \theta,r\sin\theta)\,d\theta\,dr. \end{aligned} \]

Thus, we can compute double integrals on polar rectangles by means of the following change of variables formula:

Change of Variables Formula for Polar Coordinates. Let $R$ be a polar rectangle of the form \[ R = \big\{(r,\theta)\mid a\leq r \leq b,\,\alpha\leq \theta\leq \beta\big\}, \] with $0\leq\beta-\alpha\leq 2\pi$, and $f\colon R\to\RR$ a continuous function. Then we have \[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_\alpha^\beta\int_a^b rf(r \cos \theta,r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta = \int_a^b\int_\alpha^\beta rf(r \cos \theta,r\sin\theta)\,d\theta\,dr. \]

In other words, to compute the integral in polar coordinates we set $x=r \cos\theta$ and $y=r \sin\theta$, and we multiply the whole function by $r$.

A very common mistake when computing integrals in polar coordinates is to not include the $r$ in the right hand side, so make sure to not forget it!
Compute the integral \[ \iint_R (x^2-y)\,dA, \] where $R$ is the region in the first quadrant that is bounded by the circles $x^2+y^2=1$ and $x^2+y^2=4$.
Solution.

Looking at the picture in the right, we see that this region can be described in polar coordinates as \[ R = \bigg\{(r,\theta)\mid 1\leq r\leq 2,\,0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}2\bigg\}. \] Therefore, changing to polar coordinates we obtain \[ \iint_R (x^2-y)\,dA = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\int_1^2r(r^2\cos^2\theta-r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta. \]

We compute the double integral in question: \[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\int_1^2r(r^2\cos^2\theta-r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta =& \int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\int_1^2(r^3\cos^2\theta-r^2\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\bigg[\frac{r^4}4\cos^2\theta-\frac{r^3}3\sin\theta\bigg]_{r=1}^{r=2}\,d\theta \\ =& \int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\bigg(\frac{15}{4} \cos ^2\theta -\frac{7 }{3}\sin \theta \bigg)\,d\theta = \frac{15}4\int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\cos ^2\theta\,d\theta -\frac{7 }{3}\int_0^{\frac{\pi}2}\sin \theta \,d\theta\\ =& \frac{15 \pi }{16}-\frac{7}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

Find the integral \[ \int_{-2}^2\int_{-\sqrt{4-y^2}}^0(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy \] by converting it into a double integral over a region $R$ and changing to polar coordinates.
Solution.

From the bounds of integration, we see that the iterated integral in question is \[ \int_{-2}^2\int_{-\sqrt{4-y^2}}^0(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy = \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA \] for the region \[ R=\bigg\{ (x,y)\mid -2\leq y\leq 2,\,-\sqrt{4-y^2}\leq x\leq 0 \bigg\}. \]

Looking at the picture in the right, we see that this region is given (in polar coordinates) as \[ R = \bigg\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq r\leq 2,\,\frac{\pi}2\leq\theta\leq\frac{3\pi}2\bigg\}. \] Thus, the change of variables formula gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{-2}^2\int_{-\sqrt{4-y^2}}^0(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy =& \iint_R(x^2+y^2)\,dA = \int_0^2\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{3\pi}2}r(r^2\cos^2\theta+r^2\sin^2\theta)\,d\theta\,dr \\ =& \int_0^2\int_{\frac{\pi}2}^{\frac{3\pi}2}r^3\,d\theta\,dr = \pi\int_0^2r^3\,dr = 4\pi. \end{aligned} \]
Compute the volume of the solid $S$ bounded by the plane $z=0$ and the paraboloid $z=9-x^2-y^2$.
Solution.

We see that the intersection of the plane $z=0$ and the paraboloid
$z=9-x^2-y^2$ is the circle $x^2+y^2=9$ centered at the origin and with radius $3$. Consequently, the volume of $S$ is equal to \[ \iint_R(9-x^2-y^2)\,dA, \] where $R$ is the disk centered at $(0,0)$ with radius $3$. Now, we can describe $R$ in polar coordinates as \[ R = \big\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq r\leq 3,\,0\leq \theta\leq 2\pi\big\}. \]

Now, the change of variables formula for polar coordinates yields \[ \begin{aligned} \iint_R(9-x^2-y^2),\,dA =& \int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi} r(9-r^2\cos^2\theta-r^2\sin^2\theta)\,d\theta\,dr = \int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi}r(9-r^2)\,d\theta\,dr \\ =& 2\pi\int_0^3(9r-r^3)\,dr = 2\pi\bigg[\frac92r^2-\frac14r^4\bigg]_{r=0}^{r=3} = \frac{81\pi}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Example. Find the volume of the solid $S$ that is enclosed above the cone \[z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\] and below the sphere \[x^2+y^2+z^2=4.\]
Solution.

As we see on the above picture, the sphere and the cone intersect in a circle. Let us start by finding said circle.

Since the top part of the sphere is given by the equation $z=\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}$, the points $(x,y)$ on which these two surfaces intersect are given by the equation \[ \sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}, \] which, after rearranging, becomes \[ x^2+y^2=2. \] This means that the two surfaces intersect at the circle centered at the origin with radius $\sqrt{2}$.

Now, let us compute the volume of $S$. This volume can be obtained by subtracting:

  • the volume of the solid enclosed between the sphere $z=\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}$ and the disk $D$ centered at the origin and with radius $\sqrt{2}$,
  • and the volume of the solid enclosed between the cone $z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and the same disk $D$.
Note that, in polar coordinates, \[ D=\big\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq r\leq \sqrt{2},\,0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi \big\}. \] Thus, the first volume is \[ \begin{aligned} \iint_D\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}\,dA =& \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt{2}}r\sqrt{4-r^2}\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^{\sqrt{2}}r\sqrt{4-r^2}\,dr = -\pi\int_4^2\sqrt{u}\,du \\=& -\frac{2\pi}{3}\big[u^{\frac32}\big]_{u=4}^{u=2} = \frac{2\pi}{3}(8-2\sqrt{2}), \end{aligned} \] where we have used the substitution $u=4-r^2$, whereas the second volume is \[ \begin{aligned} \iint_D\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dA =& \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt{2}}r^2\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^{\sqrt{2}}r^2\,dr = \frac{2\pi}{3}\big[r^3\big]_{r=0}^{r=2} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}\pi}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

In conclusion, the volume of $S$ is \[ \frac{2\pi}{3}(8-2\sqrt{2}) - \frac{4\sqrt{2}\pi}{3} = \frac{(16-8\sqrt{2})\pi}{3}. \]

When integrating in polar coordinates, one can also consider regions of integration that are not polar rectangles. For example, the analogues of the regions of Type I and regions of Type II are the polar regions of the form \[ D = \big\{(r,\theta)\mid \alpha\leq \theta\leq \beta,\, g_1(\theta)\leq r\leq g_2(\theta)\big\}, \] where $g_1$ and $g_2$ are continuous functions of $\theta$.

For these regions, the change of variables formula takes a similar form as in regions of Type I or Type II:

Consider a polar region of the form \[ D = \big\{(r,\theta)\mid \alpha\leq \theta\leq \beta,\, g_1(\theta)\leq r \leq g_2(\theta)\big\},. \] If $f\colon D\to \RR$ is a continuous function, then the double integral of $f$ over $D$ is \[ \iint_D f\,dA = \int_\alpha^\beta\int_{g_1(\theta)}^{g_2(\theta)} rf(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta. \]
Example. The three-leaved rose is the curve given by the equation \[r=\cos(3\theta)\] in polar coordinates. Find the area enclosed by one of its leaves.
Solution.

Let us focus on the leaf of the rose that lies to the right of the $y$-axis (that is, where $-\tfrac{\pi}2\leq \theta\ \leq \tfrac{\pi}2$). Note that the solutions to $\cos(3\theta)=0$ in the interval $\big[-\tfrac{\pi}2,\tfrac{\pi}2\big]$ are $\pm\tfrac{\pi}6$ and $\pm\tfrac{\pi}2$, so the leaf in question can be described as the polar region \[ D=\bigg\{(r,\theta)\mid -\frac{\pi}6\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}6,\,0\leq r\leq \cos(3\theta)\bigg\}. \]

Consequently, the area of this leaf is \[ \begin{aligned} A(D) =& \iint_D 1\,dA = \int_{-\frac{\pi}6}^{\frac{\pi}6}\int_0^{\cos(3\theta)}r\,dr\,d\theta = \frac12\int_{-\frac{\pi}6}^{\frac{\pi}6}\cos^2(3\theta)\,d\theta = \frac14\int_{-\frac{\pi}6}^{\frac{\pi}6}(1+\cos(6\theta))\,d\theta \\ =& \frac14\bigg[\theta+\frac16\sin(6\theta)\bigg]_{\theta=-\frac{\pi}6}^{\theta=\frac{\pi}6} = \frac{\pi}{12}. \end{aligned} \]

Example. Find the area of the region $D$ that lies outside the cardioid \[ r=2+2\sin\theta \] and inside the circle \[ r=6\sin\theta. \]
Solution.

Let us describe $D$ as a polar region. To do this, we first need to see when the two given curves intersect. We have \[ 6\sin\theta = 2+2\sin\theta \iff \sin\theta=\frac12\iff \theta=\frac{\pi}6\textsf{ or }\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}, \] since the angles of intersection lie between $0$ and $\pi$. Therefore, we can write $D$ in polar coordinates as \[ D= \bigg\{(r,\theta)\mid \frac{\pi}6\leq \theta\leq \frac{5\pi}{6},\, 2+2\sin\theta\leq r\leq 6\sin\theta\bigg\}. \]

We conclude that the area of $D$ is \[ \begin{aligned} A(D) =& \iint_D1\,dA = \int_{\frac{\pi}6}^{\frac{5\pi}6}\int_{2+2\sin\theta}^{6\sin\theta} dr\,d\theta = \int_{\frac{\pi}6}^{\frac{5\pi}6}(4\sin\theta-2)\,d\theta = \bigg[-4\cos\theta-2\theta\bigg]_{\theta=\frac{\pi}6}^{\theta=\frac{5\pi}6} \\={}&4 \sqrt{3}-\frac{4 \pi }{3}. \end{aligned} \]
