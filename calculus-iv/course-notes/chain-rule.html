The Chain Rule

Recall that the Chain Rule for functions of one variable allows us to take the derivative of composite functions. Precisely, the Chain Rule says that if $f(x)$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g(x)$ is differentiable at $f(a)$, then $(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))$ is differentiable at $a$, with \[ (g\circ f)'(a)=f'(a)g'(f(a)). \] In other words, if we are given variables $x,\,y$ and $z$ such that $y=y(x)$ depends on $x$ and $z=z(y)$ depends on $y$, then \[ \frac{dz}{dx}=\frac{dz}{dy}\frac{dy}{dx}. \] The objective of this section is to develop a version of the Chain Rule that applies to functions of several variables.

The Chain Rule: First Version

We start with a "simple case" to see what a multi-dimensional Chain Rule should look like. Take a differentiable function $z=f(x,y)$ of two variables, and assume that the variables $x$ and $y$ depend on some variable $t$. In other words, $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$. We suppose that both $x$ and $y$ are differentiable functions of $t$. We want to compute the derivative \[ \frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t)). \] To compute this, consider an increment $\Delta t$ of the variable $t$. We also write $\Delta x=x(t+\Delta t)$, $\Delta y=y(t+\Delta t)-y(t)$ and $\Delta z=f(x(t+\Delta t),y(t+\Delta t))-f(x(t),y(t))$. Since $f$ is differentiable at $(x(t),y(t))$, we have \[ \Delta z=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x +\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Delta y +\varepsilon_1 \Delta x + \varepsilon_2 \Delta y, \] where $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ go to zero as we approach $(x(t),y(t))$. Dividing by $\Delta t$, we obtain \[ \frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} +\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} +\varepsilon_1 \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} + \varepsilon_2 \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}. \] Now, as $\Delta t\to 0$, so do $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, so that $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ also tend to zero. This means that the last two terms in the above equation go to zero as $\Delta t\to 0$, and we conclude that \[ \frac{dz}{dt}=\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{d x}{d t} +\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{d y}{d t}. \]

In conclusion:

If $z=f(x,y)$ is a function of two variables $x$ and $y$, and both $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$ depend on a single variable $t$, then the derivative of $z$ with respect to $t$ is \[ \frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} +\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}. \]
If $z=\ln(2x-y^3)$, $x=\cos t$ and $y=\sin^2 t$, find $\frac{dz}{dt}$.
Solution.

Using the Chain Rule, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dz}{dt} &= \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} +\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{2}{2x-y^3}\big(-\sin t\big) +\frac{-3y^2}{2x-y^3}\cdot 2\sin(t)\cos(t) \\ &= \frac{-2\sin t-6y^2\sin (t)\cos (t)}{2x-y^3}. \end{aligned} \] Recalling that $x=\cos t$ and $y=\sin^2 t$, we arrive at \[ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{-2\sin t-6\sin t\cos^5 t}{2 \cos t-\sin^6 t}. \]

The above version of the Chain Rule can also be generalized to functions of more than two variables as follows:

Suppose $z=f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is a differentiable function of the variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ and each of the variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ is a function of the variable $t$. Then, \[ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x_1}\frac{dx_1}{dt} +\cdots+ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x_n}\frac{dx_n}{dt}. \]
Suppose $f(x,y,z)$ is a differentiable function such that $f(2,1,0)=-2$, $f_x(2,1,0)=-3$, $f_y(2,1,0)=1$ and $f_z(2,1,0)=1$. If $h(t)=f(2t,e^{t-1},\sin(\pi t))$, compute $h'(1)$.
Solution.

In this case, we have $x=2t$, $y=e^{t-1}$ and $z=\sin(\pi t)$, so the Chain Rule yields that \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dh}{dt} &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} +\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} +\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt} = 2 \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} +e^{t-1} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} +\pi \cos(\pi t) \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}. \end{aligned} \] Now, put $t=1$, so $x=2$, $y=1$ and $z=0$. Since \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(2,1,0)=-3,\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(2,1,0)=1,\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(2,1,0)=1, \end{aligned} \] we deduce that \[ \frac{dh}{dt}(1)=2\cdot(-3)+1\cdot 1+\pi\cdot(-1)\cdot 1 =-5-\pi. \]

The Chain Rule: General Version

So far, we know how to differentiate the composition of a function of $n$ variables with $n$ functions of one variable. Here we will make use of this fact to find the partial derivatives of the composition of a function of $n$ variables together with $n$ functions of $m$ variables.

Let's set the stage first. Consider a differentiable function $u=f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ that depends on $n$ variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$. Furthermore, suppose that each of the variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ depends differentiably (i.e. are differentiable functions) on $m$ variables $t_1,\,\dots,\,t_m$. In other words, we have \[ x_1=x_1(t_1,\dots,t_m),\quad x_2=x_2(t_1,\dots,t_m),\quad\dots,\quad x_n=x_n(t_1,\dots,t_m). \] Then, we can view $u$ as a function of the variables $t_1,\,\dots,\,t_m$: \[ u=u(x_1(t_1,\dots,t_m),\dots,x_n(t_1,\dots,t_m)). \] We usually say that $u$ is the dependent variable, $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ are the intermediate variables, and $t_1,\,\dots,\,t_m$ are the independent variables.

Our objective will be to compute the partial derivative of $u$ with respect to the variable $t_i$. To make the argument easier, we will assume that $t_i=t_1$ (but the argument is valid in general). To do this, consider the function \[ h(t)=u(x_1(t,t_2,\dots,t_m),\dots,x_n(t,t_2,\dots,t_m)). \] Then by definition, we have \[ h'(t_1)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t_1}, \] and by using the first version of the Chain Rule, we see that \[ \begin{aligned} h'(t_1) &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}\frac{d}{dt}x_1(t,t_2,\dots,t_m) + \cdots + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_n}\frac{d}{dt}x_n(t,t_2,\dots,t_m) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial t_1} + \cdots + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_n}\frac{\partial x_n}{\partial t_1}. \end{aligned} \]

Our conclusion is thus that \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t_1} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial t_1} + \cdots + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_n}\frac{\partial x_n}{\partial t_1}. \] With this in mind, we can now state the general version of the Chain Rule:

Suppose $u=u(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is a differentiable function of the variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ and each of the variables $x_1,\,\dots,\,x_n$ is a differentiable function of the variables $t_1,\,\dots,\,t_m$. Then $u$ is a function of $t_1,\,\dots,\,t_m$ and \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t_i} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial t_i} + \cdots + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_n}\frac{\partial x_n}{\partial t_i} \] for each variable $t_i$.
Suppose $f(x,y)$ is a differentiable function. If $x=r \cos \theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$, compute the partial derivatives \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial r} \quad\textsf{and}\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}. \]
Solution.

Using the general version of the Chain Rule, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial r} &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} = \cos \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \sin \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}, &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} = -r \sin \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + r \cos \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}. \end{aligned} \]

Let $f(x,y)$ be a differentiable function and $g(t)$, $h(t)$ two differentiable functions. Compute the partial derivatives $F_y$ and $F_z$ of \[ F(x,y,z)=f(g(x+y),h(y+z)). \]
Solution.

If we write $u=g(x+y)$ and $v=h(y+z)$, then the general version of the Chain Rule gives \[ F_y = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = g'(x+y)\frac{\partial f}{\partial u} + h'(y+z)\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}. \] Similarly, \[ F_z = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = h'(y+z)\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}. \]

If $u=x^3y-xz^2$, where $x=r+se^t$, $y=s^2e^{-t}$ and $z=r\cos t$, Find the partial derivatives \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\quad\textsf{and}\quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} \] when $r=1$, $s=2$ and $t=0$.
Solution.

Using the chain rule, we have \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u}{\partial r} &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+ \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+ \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \frac{\partial z}{\partial r} = \big(3x^2y-z^2\big)\cdot 1+x^3\cdot 0-2zx\cos t \\&= 3x^2y-z^2-2zx\cos t. \end{aligned} \] Now, if $r=1$, $s=2$ and $t=0$, then $x=3$, $y=4$ and $z=1$, so \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = 3\cdot 9\cdot 4-1^2-2\cdot 1\cdot 3\cdot \cos 0=101. \]

We now compute $\partial u/\partial t$. In this case, we have \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+ \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}+ \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = \big(3x^2y-z^2\big)\cdot se^t+x^3\cdot \big(-s^2e^{-t}\big) -2zx\big(-r\sin t\big). \end{aligned} \] Substituting when $r=1$, $s=2$ and $t=0$, we obtain \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \big(3\cdot 9\cdot 4 - 1\big)\cdot 2+27\cdot \big(-4\big) = 106. \]

Suppose $u=f(x,y)$ for a differentiable function $f$ that has continuous partial derivatives of order $2$, If $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$, Compute \[ \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial\theta\,\partial r}. \]
Solution.

We start by taking the (first) partial derivative: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} = \cos \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \sin\theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}. \end{aligned} \] Now we differentiate the above expression with respect to $\theta$, applying the chain rule to the partial derivatives of $f$ and Clairaut's theorem. \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial\theta\,\partial r} =& -\sin \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} +\cos \theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} + \cos \theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} +\cos\theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + \sin\theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} + \sin\theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}\\ =& -\sin \theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} -r\sin\theta\cos \theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + r\cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} +\cos\theta \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} -r\sin^2\theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} \\ &+r\sin\theta\cos\theta \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}\\ \end{aligned} \]
